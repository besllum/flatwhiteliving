Interior Design in The Hague’s Historic Homes

By Elisa Tienpont · 16 November 2026

The Hague has many homes with a strong architectural identity. From elegant townhouses and turn-of-the-century apartments to buildings with distinctive period details, the city offers plenty of inspiration for anyone interested in interiors.

Designing these homes requires a thoughtful approach. The aim is to understand what gives the property its character, while making sure the interior supports the way its owners want to live today.

Start with the architecture

High ceilings, decorative mouldings, original doors, fireplaces, parquet floors and generous windows can all contribute to the atmosphere of an older home. These details give a space a sense of history and proportion that can be difficult to reproduce.

Before making design decisions, I like to understand which elements should remain part of the story. Sometimes the best approach is simply to restore or highlight an existing feature. In other cases, a more contemporary intervention can make the original architecture feel even stronger.

It is not about preserving every element at all costs. It is about knowing what gives the home its identity and making decisions with that in mind.

Creating a contemporary way of living

Historic homes were not always designed around the routines of modern life. Kitchens may be separate from living spaces, storage can be limited and rooms may feel disconnected.

Interior design can help bridge that gap. A carefully considered change to the layout, bespoke joinery or a more functional kitchen can transform the way a home is used without removing its architectural character.

Where structural or other significant changes are being considered, the building's status and applicable requirements need to be checked before work begins. In a protected monument, some interior elements may themselves be part of what is protected.

Finding the right balance between old and new

I often find that contrast gives an interior its personality. A contemporary sofa can sit beautifully in a room with original cornices. A simple dining table can bring a fresh perspective to a space with traditional proportions. Modern lighting can add a different layer without competing with existing details.

Materials play an important role in connecting these elements. Natural wood, stone, linen, wool and carefully chosen metals can bring warmth and texture, while a restrained colour palette helps the architecture remain present.

The goal is not to make every element match. It is to create a relationship between them.

Working with proportion, light and detail

Older homes often have generous ceiling heights and tall windows, but that does not automatically make every room easy to furnish. Long, narrow rooms, deep floor plans or awkward transitions between spaces can require careful planning.

Furniture scale, lighting and circulation are just as important as the choice of finishes. Bespoke joinery can make use of difficult corners, create storage without visual clutter and bring a sense of order to rooms with unusual proportions.

A home with its own identity

For me, a successful interior in a historic home should feel neither like a museum nor like a completely new building placed inside an old one. It should feel natural.

Respecting the architecture does not mean being bound by it. With a clear understanding of the building and a thoughtful design approach, it is possible to create an interior that feels contemporary, personal and connected to the history of the home.
