Renovating a Home in the Netherlands as an Expat

By Elisa Tienpont · 16 November 2026

Renovating a home in a country you are still getting to know can feel like a project within a project. You are not only making decisions about layout, materials and finishes; you are also learning how the local building process works, who is involved and which requirements apply to your property.

For international homeowners in the Netherlands, preparation is especially important. A clear design and a realistic plan can make the difference between a renovation that feels manageable and one that becomes difficult to coordinate.

Start with the property, not the finishes

Before choosing a kitchen, bathroom or colour palette, understand the condition and potential of the home. The age of the building, its structure, existing installations, insulation, windows and previous alterations all influence the scope of work.

In older properties, it is particularly important to investigate what is behind the visible surfaces. If renovation or demolition work may disturb materials in a building constructed before 1994, an asbestos survey is generally required, with limited exceptions. This should be considered early, not once the contractor is ready to start.

A measured survey and, where appropriate, advice from structural or building specialists can help establish what is possible before the design is developed in detail.

Understand the Dutch building framework

The Netherlands has a national framework for building quality, alongside local planning rules. Since January 2024, the Environment and Planning Act (Omgevingswet) has brought many rules concerning the physical environment together.

For building work, two separate questions are important:

  • Does the project require permission for the technical building activity?
  • Does it require permission under the municipality's environment plan?

The answer depends on the proposed work and the location. Some activities are permit-free, but that does not mean that technical building requirements no longer apply.

The Omgevingsloket is the official starting point for checking the rules that apply to a specific address and project. For protected monuments or properties in protected townscapes, additional requirements may apply.

If you are renovating an apartment

Apartment renovations can involve another layer of approval. In the Netherlands, apartment owners are generally members of a homeowners' association, known as a VvE.

The deed of division and the VvE regulations define which parts of the building are private and which are shared. This distinction matters when considering changes to windows, façades, balconies, structural elements, shared installations or other common parts.

Do not assume that a change is permitted simply because it takes place inside your apartment. Check the relevant documents and obtain any required VvE approval before committing to the work. Municipal permission, where required, is a separate matter.

Build a realistic budget and programme

A renovation budget should account for more than construction costs. Design fees, surveys, technical advice, permits, materials, furniture, lighting and unexpected work may all need to be considered.

It is sensible to establish priorities early. Which elements are essential? Where is there flexibility? Which decisions need to be made before work begins, and which can be made later?

The programme should also allow time for surveys, design development, quotations, approvals, ordering and delivery. A start date agreed with a contractor is not the same as a confirmed completion date, especially when materials or permissions are still outstanding.

Managing a renovation from abroad

When you are not living close to the property, communication and coordination become even more important. Drawings, written specifications, meeting notes and documented decisions help keep everyone aligned.

A project manager can act as the connection between the client, designer, contractors and suppliers, following up on progress and bringing decisions to the client in a clear and organised way. This is particularly valuable when several trades need to work in sequence.

Renovating in the Netherlands does not need to be complicated, but it does require an understanding of the property, the regulations and the people involved. With a considered design and the right project structure, the process can be approached with greater clarity and confidence.
