What to Expect When Renovating a Home in the Netherlands

By Elisa Tienpont · 16 November 2026

Renovating a home in the Netherlands can be a rewarding way to make a property work better for your lifestyle. Whether you are updating an apartment, reconfiguring a family home or undertaking a complete renovation, understanding the process before you begin makes a significant difference.

Every project is different, and the scope of work will determine how much planning, technical advice and approval is required. However, most renovations follow a series of stages that connect the initial ideas with the finished home.

1. Understanding the property and your priorities

The first step is to understand both the property and the people who will live in it.

This involves looking at the existing layout, natural light, storage, installations, structural elements and the general condition of the building. It is also the moment to establish what is not working and what you would like to achieve.

For an older property, surveys and technical investigations may be necessary before deciding what can be changed. If refurbishment or demolition work may disturb materials in a building constructed before 1 January 1994, an asbestos survey is generally required, subject to limited exceptions.

Understanding the property early helps establish a realistic scope and reduces the risk of making design decisions based on assumptions.

2. Developing the design

Once the brief and existing conditions are understood, the design can begin to take shape.

This stage may include space planning, furniture layouts, lighting, material selections, colour palettes, kitchen and bathroom design, and bespoke joinery.

It is important to look at the home as a whole. A change to one room can influence the circulation, lighting or proportions of another. Thinking about these connections early helps create a coherent result.

The design should also be considered in relation to the available budget. This allows priorities to be established before detailed specifications and quotations are prepared.

3. Technical development and permissions

A design needs to be translated into information that contractors and specialists can use.

Depending on the project, this may include technical drawings, construction details, electrical and lighting plans, plumbing layouts, structural calculations and specifications.

In the Netherlands, building work is governed by the Environment and Planning Act (Omgevingswet) and the technical requirements in the Besluit bouwwerken leefomgeving (Bbl). Some activities require an environmental and planning permit; others may be permit-free or require a notification. Technical and spatial permissions are separate checks, and the answer depends on the work and location.

The official Omgevingsloket is the starting point for checking the requirements for a specific property and project.

If the property is an apartment, the homeowners' association (VvE) may also need to approve certain changes, particularly where shared building elements or installations are involved. Protected monuments and properties in protected townscapes can have additional requirements.

These checks should happen before work is scheduled.

4. Selecting contractors and confirming costs

With the design and scope sufficiently developed, contractors can be approached for quotations.

Clear drawings and specifications make it easier to obtain meaningful prices and compare offers. It is important to understand what is included, what is excluded, which materials have been priced and whether subcontractors are involved.

Once a contractor is selected, the scope, price, payment arrangements, programme, responsibilities and process for additional work should be documented in an agreement.

This is also the time to establish who is coordinating the contractors, who is responsible for technical decisions and how communication will be managed.

5. Preparing for construction

Before work begins, the project needs to be organised.

This includes confirming the programme, ordering materials, checking measurements, coordinating deliveries and ensuring that the contractors have the information they need.

The order of work is particularly important. Demolition, structural changes, electrical and plumbing installations, plastering, flooring, joinery and finishing all need to be planned in relation to one another.

Not every product can be ordered at the same time, but long-lead items should be identified early to avoid unnecessary delays.

6. Construction and coordination

Once construction starts, the design becomes a physical space. This is when coordination, communication and decision-making become part of everyday project management.

Progress needs to be followed, questions need to reach the right people and any changes should be assessed before they are implemented.

Existing buildings can reveal unexpected conditions. When this happens, the appropriate specialists and contractors should assess the issue, and the client should understand the implications for cost and timing before a solution is agreed.

The project manager's role is to help keep the different parts of the project connected and ensure that decisions are communicated clearly. This role should be distinguished from formal technical inspections or statutory supervision, which may require separate professionals.

7. Completion and handover

As the construction work comes to an end, outstanding items need to be identified and addressed. This may include adjustments, finishing details, missing installations or corrections to completed work.

A final review should be made against the agreed scope. Relevant documentation, product information, warranties and other records should also be collected.

A considered handover helps the client understand how the completed home has been delivered and what information they need for its future maintenance.

A process that needs to work as a whole

The most successful renovations are not necessarily the ones with the largest budgets or the most dramatic transformations. They are the ones where the design, technical decisions, people and programme have been considered together.
