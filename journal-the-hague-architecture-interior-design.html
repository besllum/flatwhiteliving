What Makes The Hague’s Architecture Interesting for Interior Design?

By Elisa Tienpont · 16 November 2026

One of the things I find interesting about The Hague is the variety of its architecture. The city has grown over many different periods, and that history is visible in its streets and residential neighbourhoods.

You can move from elegant historic houses to early twentieth-century residential architecture, and from traditional brick buildings to more modernist and post-war developments. This variety gives the city a distinctive character and offers many different starting points for interior design.

A city of architectural layers

The Hague is often associated with its stately townhouses, villas and historic residential streets. Neighbourhoods such as the Archipelbuurt, Statenkwartier and Duinoord have a strong architectural identity, with buildings that contribute to the character of the streets around them.

But the city's architecture is not limited to one period or style. Its development over the twentieth century brought different approaches to housing, materials and urban planning. This layering is part of what makes the city interesting: buildings from different periods sit alongside one another, each reflecting the needs and ideas of its time.

The influence of the building on the interior

Architecture provides the starting point for an interior, but it does not have to dictate every decision.

In a home with high ceilings, large windows and decorative details, I might look for ways to preserve that sense of proportion while introducing simpler furniture and contemporary lighting. In a more modernist property, the structure, geometry and natural light might become the main elements around which the interior is developed.

Understanding the building helps determine what to emphasise, what to soften and where a contrast could bring something new.

Materials, proportions and atmosphere

The materials and details of a building can also influence the interior palette. Brick, timber, stone, plaster and metal each have a different presence, and the relationship between these materials can help create a natural connection between the architecture and the furnishings.

Proportion is just as important. A large room may need substantial furniture to feel balanced, while a narrower space often benefits from carefully selected pieces that preserve circulation. The scale of lighting, artwork and joinery can all influence how a room feels.

Respecting the past without recreating it

I do not believe that an older building needs to be furnished entirely in a traditional style, or that a contemporary home should feel cold or minimal. Some of the most interesting interiors come from combining different periods and expressions.

A historic room can feel fresh with a contemporary sofa, a sculptural light or a more restrained colour palette. Equally, a modern interior can gain depth from vintage furniture, tactile materials and objects with personal meaning.

The important thing is that the choices feel connected rather than simply decorative.

Letting the building and the people shape the design

The architecture of The Hague offers a rich source of inspiration, but the people who live in a home should always remain central to the design.

I see interior design as a conversation between the building, its surroundings and the people who use it. The architecture gives us a framework; the layout, materials, furniture and details make it a home.

That balance is what makes working with the city's varied housing so rewarding.
