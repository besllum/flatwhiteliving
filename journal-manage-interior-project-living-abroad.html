How to Manage an Interior Project When You Live Abroad

By Elisa Tienpont · 16 November 2026

Renovating a home is already a complex process. Managing it from another country adds another layer: different time zones, distance from the property, unfamiliar contractors and the challenge of making important decisions without being able to visit the site regularly.

For international homeowners, this does not mean that a renovation needs to be put on hold until they move. With the right preparation, clear communication and someone coordinating the process locally, a project can move forward while you continue with your everyday life elsewhere.

Establish a clear brief from the beginning

When you cannot visit the property frequently, clarity becomes even more important.

The first step is to establish what you want to achieve, how you intend to use the home and which elements matter most. This includes the layout, storage, materials, furniture, lighting and the practical requirements of daily life.

A clear brief helps guide decisions throughout the project. It also gives the designer and contractors a shared understanding of what is being created.

Have accurate information about the property

Before making important design decisions, it is essential to understand the existing conditions.

Measured drawings, photographs, videos and, where appropriate, a site survey help build an accurate picture of the property. Technical investigations may also be necessary, particularly in older buildings or where structural changes are being considered.

Relying on approximate measurements or outdated plans can create problems later, especially with bespoke furniture, kitchens, bathrooms and built-in storage.

Appoint a local point of contact

One of the biggest challenges when managing a renovation from abroad is the number of people involved. Contractors, suppliers and specialists may need answers quickly, and some decisions cannot wait until your next visit.

Having a local project manager creates a consistent point of contact. They can coordinate communication, follow up on progress, clarify design information and bring decisions to you in an organised way.

It is important to agree exactly what this role includes. Project coordination, design management, contractor supervision, technical inspections and legal or contractual responsibilities are different services and should not be assumed to be interchangeable.

Make decisions before they become urgent

Many renovation delays are caused by decisions that are made too late.

Materials, lighting, sanitaryware, kitchen details and bespoke furniture often need to be selected and approved well before installation. When clients live abroad, the decision-making process should be organised so that choices can be reviewed without unnecessary pressure.

A decision schedule can help identify what needs to be approved, by when and what information is required. Presenting a small number of well-considered options, with clear specifications and implications, makes decisions easier.

Use documentation to keep everyone aligned

Good documentation is essential when you are not on site.

Drawings, specifications, meeting notes, photographs and written approvals provide a shared reference for the client, designer and contractors. They also help avoid misunderstandings when several people are working on different parts of the project.

A central record should include:

  • Current drawings and design information.
  • Approved materials, finishes and product selections.
  • Contractor quotations and agreed changes.
  • Budget updates and payment records.
  • Construction progress photographs.
  • Open questions and decisions awaiting approval.
  • An updated programme and delivery information.

The aim is not to create paperwork for its own sake, but to make sure that important information is easy to find and everyone is working from the latest version.

Plan site visits with purpose

Remote communication is useful, but it cannot replace every site visit.

Certain stages benefit from being reviewed in person, particularly before important elements are closed up or installed. Examples include checking structural changes, confirming dimensions for bespoke joinery, reviewing material samples and assessing the finished quality of key details.

If you cannot attend, agree in advance how these stages will be documented and who is responsible for any required technical checks.

Understand the practical requirements in the Netherlands

If the property is in the Netherlands, local requirements still need to be addressed even when the owner lives elsewhere.

Depending on the work, this may include checking environmental and planning permits, technical building requirements, VvE approval for an apartment, or additional rules for a protected property. The Omgevingsloket provides the official permit check for a specific location and proposed activity.

It is also important to make sure that contracts, payment arrangements, insurance and responsibilities are clear. Being abroad does not remove the need for the client and appointed professionals to understand their respective obligations.

Create a realistic programme

A remote renovation needs a programme that includes more than the construction period. Design development, approvals, contractor appointments, material lead times and delivery coordination all need to be taken into account.

It is also worth agreeing how often you will receive updates and what should happen if an unexpected issue arises. For example, which decisions require your approval, who can deal with urgent practical questions and how changes to the budget or timeline will be communicated?

These arrangements make the process more predictable and help avoid unnecessary interruptions.

Distance does not have to mean disconnection

Managing a renovation from abroad requires trust, organisation and clear communication. It also requires someone who understands both the design intention and the practical realities of delivering it.
