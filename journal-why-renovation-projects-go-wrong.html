Why Renovation Projects Go Wrong, and How to Prevent It

By Elisa Tienpont · 16 November 2026

Renovating a home is an exciting process, but it also involves a considerable amount of coordination. There are decisions to make, contractors to appoint, materials to order and different stages of work that need to happen in the right sequence.

When something goes wrong, it is rarely caused by one isolated mistake. More often, small uncertainties build up: a detail was never properly defined, a material was ordered too late, a quotation did not include an essential item or two contractors understood the same instruction differently.

Many of these situations can be avoided through preparation, clear communication and realistic project management.

Starting construction before the design is ready

One of the most common problems is beginning construction before the important design decisions have been made.

A layout may look simple on paper, but once the work starts, details become much more complex. The position of lighting, electrical outlets, plumbing, joinery, flooring transitions and built-in furniture all need to be considered in relation to one another.

If these decisions are left until the contractor is on site, they can lead to delays, additional costs or compromises in the finished result.

How to prevent it: Develop the design to a level that gives contractors clear information before work begins. Prepare the necessary drawings, specifications and material selections, and identify any details that still require technical input.

Choosing a contractor based only on price

A low quotation can be attractive, particularly when a renovation budget is already under pressure. However, quotations are only comparable when they cover the same scope of work.

One contractor may include preparation, removal, installation and finishing, while another may price only the main construction work. The difference can become significant once the project is underway.

How to prevent it: Compare quotations line by line. Check the scope, materials, quantities, exclusions, subcontractors, VAT, payment stages and provisional sums. Ask questions before accepting an offer and make sure the final agreement reflects what has actually been discussed.

Underestimating the budget

Construction costs are not the only expenses involved in a renovation. Design, technical advice, surveys, permits, delivery, furniture, lighting and finishing materials all contribute to the overall investment.

Unexpected conditions can also appear once existing walls, floors or ceilings are opened. Older buildings in particular may reveal issues that could not be fully assessed beforehand.

How to prevent it: Establish a realistic budget before starting and distinguish between confirmed costs, estimates and items that have not yet been priced. Keep a contingency for unforeseen work and agree how additional costs will be approved.

Poor coordination between trades

A renovation is a sequence of connected activities. The electrician needs to know where the lighting and joinery will be positioned. The carpenter needs accurate dimensions. The flooring contractor needs to understand the finished floor levels. The painter needs to know when other installations are complete.

If these trades are not coordinated, work may need to be repeated or adjusted.

How to prevent it: Establish a construction sequence and make sure each contractor has the latest drawings and information. Coordinate measurements and technical details before work begins, and keep communication between the different specialists clear.

Delayed decisions and material orders

Some materials and bespoke elements require significant lead times. Kitchens, windows, stone, custom furniture and specialist lighting may need to be ordered well before installation.

A delay in choosing one element can affect several subsequent activities.

How to prevent it: Create a decision and procurement schedule. Identify which selections are time-sensitive, confirm dimensions before ordering and check delivery dates with suppliers. Do not assume that a product is available simply because it is shown in a catalogue or online.

Changes without understanding their impact

It is natural for clients to reconsider decisions as a project develops. Sometimes a different material or layout detail genuinely improves the result.

However, even a small change can affect cost, timing or work that has already been completed.

How to prevent it: Before approving a change, understand its consequences. Confirm the revised cost, possible delay and any technical implications in writing. Make sure the relevant contractors are informed before they proceed.

Not allowing enough time for planning and approvals

A renovation programme needs to include more than the construction period. Surveys, design development, contractor quotations, material orders and any necessary permissions can all take time.

In the Netherlands, some building activities require an environmental and planning permit, while others may be exempt or subject to a notification. The rules depend on the property and the proposed work.

How to prevent it: Check the applicable requirements early, using the official Omgevingsloket and professional advice where needed. Do not make the construction start date dependent on an approval that has not yet been obtained.

The value of good project management

A renovation will always involve a degree of uncertainty, particularly when working with an existing building. Good project management cannot remove every unexpected situation, but it can help identify risks early, make responsibilities clear and prevent avoidable problems from becoming expensive ones.

At Flat White Living, I see project management as an essential part of good design. The aim is to make sure that the decisions made at the beginning are carried through carefully, and that the finished home reflects the original vision as closely as possible.
